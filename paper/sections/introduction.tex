\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large wildfires destroy homes and ecosystems, and their smoke harms health far from the fire.
Fire managers decide each day where to send crews and whom to warn, so they need to know where an
active fire will be tomorrow. Physics-based spread models answer this question, but they need
detailed fuel and weather inputs~\cite{andrews2018} that are often missing at scale. Machine learning offers a
data-driven alternative~\cite{jain2020}.

We study next-day wildfire spread as defined by Huot et al.~\cite{huot2022}: given 12 input
channels on day $t$, predict for every pixel whether it is burning on day $t+1$. Their Next Day
Wildfire Spread dataset contains 18\,545 patches of U.S.\ fires from 2012 to 2020, each covering
$64\times64$\,km at 1\,km resolution with weather, terrain, vegetation, population and the day-$t$
fire mask.

The task is hard for two reasons. First, fire is rare: only 1.1\% of labelled training pixels burn
on day $t+1$, a 90:1 imbalance, so accuracy is meaningless and we report PR AUC. Second, the
labels are noisy: clouds and smoke hide part of the fire mask, and these uncertain pixels must be
left out of training and evaluation.

Our approach keeps a simple per-pixel model and moves the spatial reasoning into the features. We
build a pixel table with every fire pixel and a 5:1 sample of no-fire pixels, add 23 engineered
spatial features to the 12 raw channels, such as the distance to the nearest fire and fire upwind,
and train XGBoost and LightGBM tuned on eval PR AUC (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Tuned XGBoost
reaches a test PR AUC of 0.257 against 0.106 for persistence.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a per-pixel pipeline with 23 engineered spatial features that reaches a test PR AUC of 0.257
  (95\% bootstrap interval over fire events 0.19--0.33), between the random forest (0.225) and the
  CNN (0.284) reported by Huot et al.;
\item ablation, tuning and learning-curve results showing that the signal lies in the fire
  neighbourhood and that the model is limited by bias, not variance;
\item an error and event-level analysis showing where the model fails: spread beyond 5\,km and
  false alarms around dying fires;
\item data findings that matter for any model on this dataset: the patches are stored south-up,
  the raw export contains impossible values, and the test split contains duplicate patches.
\end{itemize}
